    How the model works
    Alertness = 100 × ( 0.64 × (1 − S) + 0.36 × C ) − W + K clipped to 0–100
    S: sleep pressure
    Two-process model (Borbély, Daan and Beersma 1984). Rises toward 1 while awake with a time constant of 18.2 h and falls toward 0 in sleep with a time constant of 4.2 h. Sleep clears pressure at 55% to 100% of full speed depending on circadian phase (slowest in your body-clock day), and caffeine in your system trims it further. Falling asleep takes 8 to 30 minutes, longer when the circadian rhythm is high and pressure is low, and that time counts as awake. The chart starts at the steady-state value after your usual bedtime and wake time.
    C: circadian rhythm
    A 24 h wave plus a 12 h harmonic for the early-afternoon dip, scaled 0 to 1. The lowest point sits 1 h after the core-temperature minimum, which is placed 2.5 h before your usual wake time. The 0.64 / 0.36 weights come from the relative size of the homeostatic and circadian terms in the two-process model (swings of about 0.43 and 0.24).
    W: sleep inertia
    After each wake-up, up to 22 points are subtracted, fading with a 0.6 h time constant. The amount grows with sleep length as 1 − e^(−hours / τ), where τ = 1.8 − 1.3 × (sleep pressure when you fell asleep). With high pressure you reach deep sleep sooner, so a short sleep after a long wake costs more than the same nap after a short wake. It is also worse when you wake near the circadian low. Sleep stages are not modelled.
    Plan sleep
    Takes your van pickup time and get-ready time as the latest wake-up. With no flight ahead it uses a wake-up time you choose and aims at the 8 hours after it. It runs the full model for hundreds of sleep windows (start time and length) that fit between your earliest bedtime and that deadline, and scores each one on alertness from pickup to landing: half the average, half the lowest point. A window cannot run past the point where sleep pressure falls to its usual wake-up level, because you would wake on your own. It only knows what the model knows, so it will not see a noisy room or a meal.
    Warnings
    Shaded amber where, while you are awake, sleep pressure is above 70% (roughly 20 hours since a normal night) or alertness, before sleep inertia, is below 25. Shading, the amber bar in the sleep row and the warning box look ahead from now to 6 hours after your last landing, so the past is never shaded. Slide a sleep block over an amber stretch to fix it. The list under the readout keeps every flagged stretch, past and future, with the past in grey. A planned sleep that fixes the problem makes the warning go away.
    While asleep
    The dotted stretch of the alertness line is how alert you would be if you were awake at that moment. It is not a measure of the sleep itself. The value after waking can sit below the value at bedtime because bedtime is often near your body-clock peak and wake-up is often near its low, plus sleep inertia. Compare it with what the line would show had you stayed awake, not with the bedtime value.
    K: caffeine
    One-compartment model for a 75 kg adult: absorption rate 3 per hour, half-life 5 h, distribution volume 45 L. The boost is up to +16 points at a typical sleep pressure (half of that at 3 mg/L, which is roughly a 130 mg dose at its peak). It is scaled by 0.5 + sleep pressure, because caffeine blocks the adenosine that builds up while you are awake: about 0.7× when rested and 1.3× after a long wake. The same concentration cuts sleep recovery by up to 30%.
    Body-clock shifts
    Light drives the shift through a phase response curve centred on your usual daylight window at home. Light earlier in your body-clock day than usual advances the clock, and light later than usual delays it. Light level is your daytime exposure setting in daylight, 20% of that when awake at night, and zero when asleep. Daylight comes from the sun's position at your location. The shift rate is normalised so that your usual day at home causes no drift. Delays are weighted 25% stronger than advances, so westward travel adjusts faster than eastward. With 50% light the model gives roughly 0.9 h/day eastward and 1.1 h/day westward for a 5 h change, in line with the usual 1 h/day and 1.5 h/day rules of thumb.
    Clock gap
    The difference between where your body clock sits and the clock where you are. When you are behind (the local clock is later than your body clock, as after flying east) the area between the two curves is red. When you are ahead (as after flying west) it is green. Gaps under 15 minutes are not shaded.
    Where you are
    You start in the zone you pick. During a flight your position moves in a straight line from the departure city to the landing city, and the local clock and the sun move with it. Each zone uses a representative city: New York for US Eastern, Dallas for US Central, London for London, and so on. A custom offset assumes 40°N.
    Night and low
    Night is shaded when the sun is below the horizon at your position. Body-clock low marks the lowest tenth of the circadian wave.
